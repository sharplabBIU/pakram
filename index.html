About the division

Psychology, Cognition, Emotion & Brain

PAKRAM (פק״רם) is the Hebrew acronym for Psychology, Cognition, Emotion and Brain, the research-oriented M.A. track of the Department of Psychology at Bar-Ilan University. Cognitive, emotional and behavioral processes in the brain unfold in continuous interaction, and together they drive human behavior. The division studies those links in depth, from basic theory to applied research.

Students learn to record brain activity in real time, analyze physiological responses and run behavioral experiments with modern sensing technologies, including eye tracking, EEG and MEG, brain stimulation (TMS and tDCS), electrophysiological recording, cutting-edge virtual reality with the Somnium VR1 headset, and computational modelling. Every student completes a thesis in a department lab under the close supervision of a faculty member, and many take part in research projects with partner hospitals and research institutes.

What makes the division distinctive is the pairing of rigorous measurement with formal modeling. Alongside classic cognitive neuroscience, our faculty bring computational psychiatry, social neuroscience, developmental science and the study of emotion into a single program, and increasingly ask what a science of the human mind can teach the people who build artificial intelligence.

Our approach

Why multidisciplinary science is the route to mechanism

The division's defining commitment is that rigorous, mechanistic models of how humans think and feel cannot be built from inside a single discipline. A mechanism is a claim about how parts interact to produce a phenomenon, and the parts of human thought and feeling live at different levels: ion channels and brain rhythms, learning rules and value computations, developing concepts, bodies in contact, and the social groups that shape all of them. A model that explains anxiety only at the level of oscillations, or only at the level of beliefs, is not yet a mechanism.

That is why the division deliberately brings computational psychiatry, magnetoencephalography and electrophysiology, developmental science, social neuroscience, neuropsychology and pharmacology into one program. Formal models give neural data a hypothesis to test; neural data constrain models that would otherwise fit behavior equally well; developmental and social perspectives reveal where a mechanism comes from and what it is for. Each discipline supplies a check on the others, and the result is explanation that is both precise and faithful to the complexity of a human life.

For students, this means learning to move between levels: to write a reinforcement-learning model in the morning, analyze MEG source estimates in the afternoon, and ask in a seminar how both bear on a clinical question or on the design of an artificial agent. This fluency is what separates a scientist who can build a mechanism from one who can only describe a correlation, and it is equally valuable in a research lab, a clinic or an R&D team.

Measure

Record the brain and body directly: MEG and EEG oscillations, brain stimulation, eye tracking, hormones and physiology, and immersive virtual reality that places people in controlled yet lifelike situations.

Model

Turn verbal theories into equations: reinforcement learning, Bayesian and active inference, and machine learning that make quantitative predictions about choices and neural signals.

Situate

Ask where a mechanism comes from and what it does in the world: in childhood, in the dyad and the group, in clinical conditions, and in artificial agents.

Two pathways, one training

Prepared for industry and for academia

The same core skills, careful experimental design, quantitative modeling and the analysis of rich neural and behavioral data, are in demand inside and outside the university. The program is built so that graduates can choose either road, or move between them.

Industry

Into industry and applied R&D

Graduates leave with a portfolio of transferable skills that technology, health and defense employers actively recruit for:

  • Programming and data analysis in Python and R, including machine learning and statistical modeling.
  • Signal processing of EEG, MEG, eye-tracking and physiological data.
  • Computational modeling of decision-making, learning and emotion, the same tools used in AI and behavioral analytics.
  • Experimental design, A/B-testing logic and human-centered evaluation for product, UX and neurotechnology teams.
  • Practica and projects that connect students with hospitals, research institutes and R&D groups.

Academia

For students who want to stay in science, the division is a direct on-ramp to doctoral research:

  • A full research thesis supervised one-on-one by a faculty member, often leading to a conference presentation or publication.

In a department survey of track graduates, 85% held research positions.

A core theme

What is computational psychiatry?

Computational psychiatry uses mathematical models of brain and behavior to understand, predict and ultimately treat mental illness. Rather than describing symptoms alone, it asks which computations have gone awry: how a person learns from reward and punishment, how they weigh uncertainty, how far ahead they plan, and how beliefs about the self and the world are updated by evidence.

The field has two complementary strands. Theory-driven approaches build explicit models, such as reinforcement learning, Bayesian inference and active inference, and fit them to a person's choices and brain signals to estimate interpretable parameters like learning rates or the precision of prior beliefs. Data-driven approaches apply machine learning to large clinical, behavioral and neural datasets to find patterns that predict diagnosis, course and treatment response. The most promising work combines the two.

Why does this matter? Psychiatric diagnoses group together people whose underlying mechanisms may differ widely, and treatments are often chosen by trial and error. Computational phenotypes, parameters that can be measured in a twenty-minute task, offer a way to identify mechanisms that cut across diagnostic categories, to track how they change with treatment, and to design interventions that target them directly.

In our division, students learn to build and fit these models themselves. A dedicated computational psychiatry practicum walks students through task design, model specification, parameter estimation and model comparison, with applications to anxiety, compulsivity, depression and more.

Emotion science meets artificial intelligence

Why a science of human emotion matters for building AI

Modern AI systems learn from reward, plan over possible futures and act under uncertainty, exactly the problems that emotions evolved to solve in biological agents. Fear reallocates attention and shortens planning horizons; curiosity drives exploration; mood summarizes whether the world is going better or worse than expected and adjusts learning accordingly. Treating emotions as computations, rather than as noise to be engineered away, gives AI researchers a principled source of design ideas and gives psychologists a formal language for what emotions do.

This is the premise of NeuroAI, the growing exchange between neuroscience and artificial intelligence. Landmark papers have argued that the brain remains the best existing proof that general intelligence is possible, and that mechanisms discovered in neuroscience, from replay and attention to intrinsic motivation and meta-learning, have repeatedly inspired advances in machine learning. Our division extends this conversation to affect: how emotion shapes valuation, learning and social behavior, and what that implies for the design, alignment and evaluation of artificial agents.

Courses in the division examine both directions of this exchange. Students read the primary literature on neuroscience-inspired AI, learn how reinforcement learning and Bayesian models formalize emotional processes, and discuss how affective science can inform safer, more human-compatible AI, including the limits of inferring emotion from faces, voices and text.

Division courses on emotion and AI treat emotions as computations that guide learning, valuation and planning in natural and artificial agents, drawing on computational psychiatry, affective neuroscience and the NeuroAI literature.

A tailor-made path

Your program, built around you

No two students arrive with the same background or leave toward the same destination. From the first semester, each student works with a faculty mentor to assemble a plan: a thesis lab, a sequence of seminars and a set of practica that build exactly the skills their path requires.

Choose a lab and a mentor

Applicants rank three potential supervisors from the department list. Once admitted, your supervisor becomes your guide to the program, the thesis and the career decisions beyond it.

Assemble your toolkit

Two research practica (4 credits each) and six elective courses let you combine, for example, computational modeling with MEG analysis, or social-neuroscience methods with machine learning.

Produce original research

The thesis is a genuine research project. Many students present at international conferences or publish before they graduate, whether they are heading to a PhD or to industry.

Computational psychiatry practicum

Design behavioral tasks, specify reinforcement-learning and Bayesian models, fit them to data and compare them, with applications to anxiety, compulsivity and mood.

  • Model building and model fitting in Python
  • Task design and online data collection
  • Critical reading of the primary literature

MEG analysis practicum

Hands-on training in magnetoencephalography at the Gonda Multidisciplinary Brain Research Center, from data acquisition to source-level analysis of brain oscillations.

  • Preprocessing and artifact rejection
  • Time-frequency and source analysis (e.g., MNE-Python)
  • Experimental design for MEG and EEG

Cutting-edge tools

A rotating practicum in the methods that define contemporary cognitive and social neuroscience.

  • Eye tracking, EEG, TMS and tDCS
  • Immersive virtual reality (Somnium VR1)
  • Computational modelling and machine learning for behavioral and neural data
  • Interpersonal synchrony and physiological recording
Admissions

Join the division

The track is a two-year M.A. with thesis, taught over two intensive days per week.

Requirements

  • A bachelor's degree in psychology with a grade average of 85 or higher.
  • Two academic or research recommendations.
  • A personal research-admissions interview.
  • Graduates of related fields (neuroscience, cognition, behavior, communication disorders) may apply and complete bridging courses.

Registration for the 2026–27 academic year runs from February 1 to August 1, 2026. Applicants pay the registration fee on the university site, complete registration in the department system, update recommender details, upload the track questionnaire and rank three potential supervisors from the supervisor list.
